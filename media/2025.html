LjósBeinnemaPáka
by Gulli Björnsson
performed by PLOrk

This piece was composed for the Princeton Laptop Orchestra and is an expansion of an installation Gulli developed in collaboration with Dr. Liam Elliott for House of Sound 2024. The piece is designed for five performers, each playing this instrument Gulli calls LjósBeinnemaPáka-IlluminatedBoneconductionTimpani a combination of a: phone’s gyroscope, bone conduction speaker, timpani and LED lights.

A bone conduction speaker is a specialized type of speaker that transmits sound vibrations through solid surfaces rather than through air. Instead of using a traditional diaphragm, it vibrates against a medium, in this case the timpani head, causing it to resonate and produce sound. This is a technology that was in part developed so a skull/skeleton could transmit sound to the inner ear, f.x. allowing people to still hear sounds in very noisy environments. Performers play the instrument in three primary ways:
  • With their phone (usually in one hand). Using a ChucK script in WebChucK, the phone’s gyroscope data modulates sine wave parameters.
    • X-axis: Selects pitch from a just intonation major scale, based on A = 440hz
    • Y-axis: Introduces slight detuning to two sine waves around the center frequency.
    • Z-axis: Controls volume.
  • With the bone conduction speaker (usually in the other hand). When placed on the timpani, the speaker turns the drumhead into a resonator/speaker. By varying pressure, performers can control the volume. Moving the speaker across the drumhead creates bowing effects, similar to friction mallets. Performers follow geometric shapes (e.g., circles, squares) shown to them in a video score to influence bowing textures.
  • With the foot pedal.The timpani’s pedal alters the drum’s tension, allowing for pitch-bend. This modifies how the drumhead interacts with the frequencies produced by the bone conduction speaker, resulting in new resonances and nodal points.
Although optional, the LED lights provide a visual representation of the music for both performers and the audience. Ideally, an audio-reactive light source is placed inside each timpani, but it is OK if it is not audio reactive.
Improvisation
by the Princeton Acoustic Laptop Orchestra
performed by Myra Norton, Liam Elliot, and Ellie Cherry

This semester, students in the course Handmade Sound Practices have been designing and building instruments and other sound making devices. One of our projects has been Acoustic Laptops. These consist of a resonant box, various soundmaking objects, a contact microphone, and an amplifier. With these instruments we explore the hidden sounds of objects within each of our unique instruments.
Carp
by Liam Elliot
performed by PLOrk

This piece pairs Resin - an electroacoustic string feedback resonator that Liam has been developing - with software instruments based on Karplus-Strong string synthesis. This synthesis technique is one of the original forms of physical modelling synthesis and uses a short delay to create string-like sounds. Performers are able to continually activate the virtual strings using contact microphones. The pitch material of this piece is generative, while the rhythms and timbres are improvised by PLOrk performers.
Breath
by Myra Norton + PLOrk
performed by PLOrk

Using Totem, an app created by Brooklyn-based creative Avery Bedows, and a MAX patch collaboratively created by PLOrk members, participants perform this piece by simply breathing. As each person breathes in and out, they scrub through a slowed and pitched recording of their own name. The sonification of breath facilitates meditation for the performers while allowing the audience to experience the rhythms generated as the PLOrk members ground themselves.
In Line
by Jascha Narveson
performed by PLOrk

Jascha was lying awake one morning picturing people playing the tether controllers like string-basses, and then imagined them gliding to different notes instead of plucking. The eventual shape of the instrument and the piece came by playing around with ideas in SuperCollider and building things up intuitively. The end result is nothing like playing string-basses. It turned into an instrument that plays pitched pulses, with the right hand’s side-to-side movement controlling pitch and speed tied together: higher is faster, lower is slower. The other five hand directions adjust everything else about the sound, and there’s some fun accidental choreography that comes out of playing this way. Starting with this instrument and working with the PLOrk class and Sideband Jascha added the ability for copies of this instrument to “be aware” of each other over a network, and have things happen if people happened to match up in pitch and timing. The piece turned into a listening game, where your instrument can “getstuck” again if you land on the same beat, turning your instrument into a rich droning sound. You can always “reset” by dropping the tethers and picking them up again. Over time Sideband developed a form for playing In Line, but other forms are possible.
